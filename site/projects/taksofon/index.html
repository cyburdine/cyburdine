:: STATUS — this one is still on the bench. Everything below is true as of today and some of it will be wrong by next month. I would rather publish the build log while it is still a build than wait for the tidy retrospective. My brother came home with a payphone. Not a replica, not a prop — an AMT-69, the real thing, out of Kazakhstan by way of a friend of his who used to live there. It had the weight that old telecom hardware has, the weight of a thing built by an organisation that assumed it would be bolted to a wall for thirty years and never thought about once. Steel, enamel, a rotary dial, a coin path worn smooth by other people’s money. He and I own a bar — The Black Hart of St. Paul. And a payphone that has been silent for decades, hung on a wall in a room full of people, is not a decoration. It is a question waiting to be answered. So the plan: make it work again. Not restore it — make it do something it was never built to do. // PUT A COIN IN. DIAL A NUMBER. HEAR SOMETHING YOU WERE NOT EXPECTING. :: THE STRIP-DOWN The first pass was subtraction. An AMT-69 is full of apparatus for a job that no longer exists: line interface, the mechanisms that talked to an exchange that has not existed in a generation, relays for billing rules nobody is enforcing any more. All of it came out. What I kept was what a person actually touches. The handset. The dial. The coin path. The body. Everything a hand meets on the way through is original; everything behind it is new. That division has held up as the organising rule of the whole build — if you can feel it, it is 1969; if you cannot, it is doing whatever I need it to do. :: THE COIN MECHANISM This is the part that fought me, and the part I am proudest of. The original coin mechanism was built around coins that are not in anyone’s pocket in Saint Paul, and around a set of assumptions about what happens after the coin drops that no longer apply. Rebuilding it meant designing a custom part and printing it — a piece that lives in the middle of a mechanism designed in the Soviet Union in the sixties and has to cooperate with tolerances I did not choose and cannot change. You do not get to redesign around a problem here. The body is the body. The part has to fit the machine, not the other way round. :: THE BRAIN A Raspberry Pi Zero, on a mounting plate I printed to fit the cavity the original electronics vacated. Audio goes out through a MAX98357 — an I2S class-D amplifier — and into the original handset earpiece. That detail matters more than it sounds: the voice you hear arrives through a 1969 receiver, with all the bandwidth limits and coloration that implies. I could have put a clean modern speaker in the shell. It would have been easier and it would have been worse. The handset’s narrow, slightly boxy sound *is* the effect. Anything hi-fi would break the spell instantly. I also printed a coin bucket, because coins have to land somewhere, and because where they land turns out to be the whole point. :: WHAT IT DOES You pick up the handset. You put in a coin. You dial a number. And someone talks to you — messages recorded by celebrities and notable people, each one sitting behind a number you have to know or guess or be told by someone else at the bar. There is no menu. There is no screen. The only interface is a rotary dial and whatever you have heard about which numbers are worth trying. The coins go to a local charity. That was true from the first sketch and it is the reason the coin mechanism had to work properly rather than just click convincingly. A coin slot that is theatre would make the donation theatre too. :: WHAT I AM STILL CHEWING ON The idea I keep coming back to: letting people *leave* messages. Dial a number, hear a tone, record something, and it sits there for whoever dials that number next. A payphone as a dead drop. That is a very different machine from the one I am currently building. It turns a jukebox into a place where strangers talk to each other across time, unsupervised, in a bar. Which is either the best idea I have had this year or a moderation problem with a rotary dial attached. Probably both. I have not decided, and I am not going to decide until the first version is on the wall and I have watched people use it. :: NEXT Wall mount. Final wiring. A number plan worth memorising. Then the part I am actually looking forward to — standing at the other end of the bar watching someone pick up a Soviet payphone in Minnesota to find out who is on the other end. // build log continues.
