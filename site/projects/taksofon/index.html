:: STATUS — this one is still on the bench. Everything below is true as of today and some of it will be wrong by next month. I would rather publish the build log while it is still a build than wait for the tidy retrospective. My brother came home with a payphone. Not a replica, not a prop — an AMT-69, the real thing, out of Kazakhstan by way of a friend of his who used to live there. It had the weight that old telecom hardware has, the weight of a thing built by an organisation that assumed it would be bolted to a wall for thirty years and never thought about once. Steel, enamel, a rotary dial, a coin path worn smooth by other people’s money. He and I own a bar — The Black Hart of St. Paul. And a payphone that has been silent for decades, hung on a wall in a room full of people, is not a decoration. It is a question waiting to be answered. // PUT A COIN IN. DIAL A NUMBER. HEAR SOMETHING YOU WERE NOT EXPECTING.
:: THE MISTAKE I MADE FIRST The coin mechanism was the whole problem, and for the first few days I was solving the wrong version of it. The phone wants kopecks. We want quarters. A quarter is bigger. So I started where I think most people would start: how do I make the existing mechanism accept a larger coin? I was sketching metal parts. I was working out which original components I could modify without destroying them, which ones I could shim, which ones I could persuade. I spent days on that. And the reason it was hard is that it was the wrong question. What eventually landed is that I was trying to cooperate with a machine that no longer had a job. All that beautiful mechanism — the linkages, the parts that had to move in a particular order — existed to satisfy an electromechanical telephone exchange that has not existed in a generation. It was solving for billing. For signalling to a central office. For a whole conversation with infrastructure that is gone. None of that is load-bearing any more. So I stopped adapting and started deleting. And once the requirement is written down honestly, it collapses to three signals: // 1. A COIN WENT IN // 2. THE HANDSET IS UP, OR IT IS DOWN // 3. THESE ARE THE NUMBERS BEING DIALLED That is the entire machine. Everything else in there was answering a question nobody is asking. Once I could see that, the coin mechanism stopped being a restoration problem and became a design problem — I was not modifying anything, I was building a new one from scratch that only had to do one job.
:: FIVE FAILURES Knowing what to build and being able to build it are different. It took five versions before the printed part worked. Six tries total. The gap is measurement. You put calipers on a sixty-year-old steel casting, you write down the numbers, you go build the shape in CAD — and then you have to hold a fraction of a millimetre across a part that has to drop into a space you cannot redesign. Every version was close. Close does not run coins. A coin either falls through the path cleanly or it hangs up, and the difference between those is smaller than the error you make transferring a physical object into 3D space in your head. Measure. Print. Doesn’t fit. Measure again. Find the place you were half a millimetre out. Print. Doesn’t fit for a different reason. The sixth one worked. Coin in, coin through, switch clicks, coin lands in the bucket. I have written software that took less out of me and gave less back — that was a genuinely magical moment, standing there feeding the same quarter through over and over just to watch it work. I also had to file down the coin slot on the face of the phone. It is cut for a kopeck, and a quarter simply does not go in. So there is now a hand-filed slot on the front of a Soviet payphone, which is either vandalism or restoration depending on how you feel about it. I feel fine about it. Nobody was going to feed it kopecks in Saint Paul.
:: HOW THE PI READS THE DIAL This is my favourite part of the build, because it turns out a rotary dial is not an input device at all. It has no electronics in it. There is nothing to interface with. What it has is a spring and a cam. You pull the finger hole round to a number and let go; the spring drags the dial back; and on the way back, a cam taps a switch open and shut — once per number. Dial a 3 and the switch clicks three times. Dial a 7 and it clicks seven times. Dial 0 and it clicks ten, which is exactly why 0 sits at the end of the dial rather than the start. So the Pi does not “read” anything. It counts blips on a wire. The switch goes to GPIO17. The Pi holds that line high with an internal pull-up and watches it; every time the dial’s switch breaks the connection the voltage jumps, an interrupt fires, and the handler does exactly one thing — increment a counter and remember when it happened. Two details keep that honest. // DEBOUNCE. A mechanical switch does not click cleanly. It chatters for a few thousandths of a second, and a naive counter reads one physical click as five. Anything inside 5ms of the last edge is ignored. // THE GAP. This is the interesting one. The Pi has no idea when a digit ends. The pulses in a 7 are identical to the pulses in a 3 followed by a 4 — same wire, same switch, same seven clicks. The only thing separating them is time: pulses inside one digit arrive fast, about ten a second, because that is how fast the spring returns the dial. Between digits there is a pause while your finger travels back to the dial. So the rule is a timeout — once a quarter-second passes with no new clicks, whatever has been counted is the digit.
Seven pulses, digit 7. Ten or more, digit 0. Digits stack into a buffer. When the buffer is as long as the number it is watching for, it compares: match plays the payoff, miss plays an error and resets. The other two pins are the same trick with less arithmetic. GPIO22 is the hook switch — lifted or hung up, nothing to count. GPIO23 is the coin switch, where one click is one quarter. That is the whole thing. Count electrical blips; use a pause to decide where one digit ends and the next begins. Which is precisely what a telephone exchange did in 1950, except that was a room full of relays and this is about forty lines of Python. :: THE BRAIN A Raspberry Pi Zero, on a mounting plate I printed to fit the cavity the original electronics left behind. Audio goes out through a MAX98357 — an I2S class-D amplifier — and into the original handset earpiece. That detail matters more than it sounds. The voice arrives through a 1969 receiver, with every bandwidth limit and coloration that implies. I could have put a clean modern speaker in the shell. It would have been easier and it would have been worse: the handset’s narrow, boxy sound is the effect, and anything hi-fi breaks the spell on contact. I printed a coin bucket too, because coins have to land somewhere, and where they land turns out to be the point.
:: WHAT IT DOES You pick up the handset. You put in a quarter. You dial a number. And someone talks to you — messages recorded by celebrities and notable people, each sitting behind a number you have to know, or guess, or be told by someone else at the bar. There is no menu. There is no screen. The only interface is a rotary dial and whatever you have heard about which numbers are worth trying. The coins go to a local charity. That was true from the first sketch, and it is the reason the coin mechanism had to actually work rather than just click convincingly. A coin slot that is theatre would make the donation theatre too. :: WHAT I AM STILL CHEWING ON The idea I keep coming back to: letting people leave messages. Dial a number, hear a tone, record something, and it sits there for whoever dials that number next. A payphone as a dead drop. That is a very different machine from the one I am currently building. It turns a jukebox into a place where strangers talk to each other across time, unsupervised, in a bar. Which is either the best idea I have had this year or a moderation problem with a rotary dial attached. Probably both. I am not deciding until the first version is on the wall and I have watched people use it. :: NEXT Wall mount. Final wiring. A number plan worth memorising. Then the part I am actually looking forward to — standing at the other end of the bar watching someone pick up a Soviet payphone in Minnesota to find out who is on the other end. // build log continues.
